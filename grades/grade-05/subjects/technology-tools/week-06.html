Week 06 · Technology & Tools

Long Division and Remainder Decisions

How do we decide what a remainder means in context?

Evidence Studio · Week 06

Essential question: How do we use accurate tables, graphs and remainder decisions to defend a practical plan?

Study: In a spreadsheet, store a number separately from its unit label so calculations remain numeric. A remainder indicates what is left after whole groups are formed. Depending on the context, it may require an extra container, remain unused, or be expressed as a fraction or decimal. Always label graph axes and identify data sources.

Worked model: A school has 137 donated books; each box holds at most 12. Long division gives 137 ÷ 12 = 11 remainder 5 because 12 × 11 = 132 and 137 − 132 = 5. Eleven boxes cannot hold all 137 books: 12 boxes are needed. Build a two-column table showing boxes 1–11 each with 12 books and box 12 with 5. A bar graph with “Box number” and “Books” labels reveals the final partial box.

Spreadsheet reasoning: Enter the total (137) and capacity (12) in separate cells. Write a calculation plan for whole boxes, remainder and total boxes, and manually check each result: 11 full boxes; 5 left; 12 needed. If an offline spreadsheet is available, use integer quotient and remainder functions after checking their syntax; paper is equally acceptable.

Guided critique: A report recommends 11 boxes because 137 ÷ 12 is about 11.42. Explain why rounding down loses 5 books; why rounding up works here; and why a class average such as 10 ÷ 3 ≈ 3.33 should not automatically be rounded up to 4 when reporting a measured mean.

Independent investigation: Repeat with 95 books and boxes of 8. Show 95 = 8 × 11 + 7, determine the 12 boxes required, make a labeled table and sketch a graph. Include a missing-value check and state whether the numbers are supplied examples or observations.

Challenge: Compare transporting 95 books using capacity-8 boxes versus capacity-12 boxes. For capacity 12: 95 = 12 × 7 + 11, so 8 boxes are needed. Which plan uses fewer boxes? Discuss one tradeoff, such as box weight or availability, without assuming those values are known.

Evidence and defense: Submit both calculations, units, labeled visualization, a corrected rounding explanation, and one limitation of the comparison. This remains instructional evidence and does not change formal scores, mastery thresholds, or teacher-review rules.
